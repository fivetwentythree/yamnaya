Yamnaya formation and launch Recent studies converge on a multi-stage process: Caucasus–Lower Volga ancestry mixed with North Pontic/Dnipro-related ancestry before a rapid Yamna expansion near 3300 BCE. Lazaridis et al. (2025); Nikitin et al. (2025); Chintalapati et al. (2022); Mathieson et al. (2018) The exact core formation zone, unsampled source populations and routes toward Anatolia remain unresolved and model-dependent. Strongest in the North Pontic, Volga and Russian Steppe datasets, but sampling is uneven. Earlier formation (~4500–4000 BCE) plus direct target-period launch (~3300 BCE) Explicit in Lazaridis and Chintalapati; indirect in Nikitin. Language remains an interdisciplinary interpretation. More direct lower-Volga, Don/Dnipro and Anatolian-route samples are needed. RETAIN — recurring 4 Migration foundation + language inference Uses ancestry formation and chronology only where they explain later movement; it does not create a separate origins section or treat genetic ancestry as language.
Westward expansion into Europe Ancestry proportions, population turnover and IBD all support rapid movement of Yamnaya-related people into central and northern Europe around 3000 BCE. Haak et al. (2015); Allentoft et al. (2015); Ringbauer et al. (2024); Papac et al. (2021) Known Yamnaya samples are not necessarily the sole direct source; Corded Ware populations rapidly incorporated local ancestry and were not socially homogeneous. Strong for Germany, Bohemia and several Corded Ware groups; less even farther south and west. Directly within 3300–2600 BCE, with immediate later transformations Explicit in Haak and Allentoft; indirect in Ringbauer and Papac. Precise migration corridors, source substructure and the balance of mass migration versus repeated smaller movements remain uncertain. RETAIN — recurring 4 Migration pattern Treats population-genetic change as evidence of westward movement, while allowing for local admixture and source uncertainty.
Sex-biased movement and local mobility Multiple studies support male-biased Steppe ancestry alongside incorporation of local women; isotope and kinship evidence shows that migration operated through local communities. Scorrano et al. (2021); Linderholm et al. (2020); Papac et al. (2021) The strength of sex bias varies by region and archaeological group; X-chromosome estimates have large uncertainty and low SNP counts. Best resolved in central Europe and southeastern Poland. Mostly ~2900–2400 BCE; spans the target period and immediate aftermath Indirect demographic mechanism only; no study identifies the language spoken by migrants or local spouses. Larger balanced cemetery samples and stronger isotope baselines are needed outside central Europe. RETAIN — recurring 3 Migration mechanism Explains how movement operated through sex bias, kinship and local incorporation; it does not assume one mechanism across all regions.
Eastward expansion into Inner Asia Afanasievo’s close relationship to Yamnaya and the appearance of this ancestry in the Altai, western Mongolia and Xinjiang support an eastward dispersal after ~3000 BCE. Wang et al. (2021); Allentoft et al. (2015); Chintalapati et al. (2022) Steppe ancestry later declined in parts of Mongolia, and an Afanasievo archaeological association did not always correspond to Yamnaya ancestry. Good broad signal across the Altai and western Mongolia, but sparse fine-scale sampling and route intermediates. Target-period movement followed by later Bronze/Iron Age persistence and turnover Explicit proto-Tocharian interpretation in Wang; broader Indo-European compatibility in Allentoft and Chintalapati. Intermediate-route samples and tighter chronology are needed to connect demographic movement to specific Tocharian branches. RETAIN — recurring 3 Migration pattern + language inference Uses eastward demographic evidence as compatible with language-dispersal hypotheses; it does not identify a spoken language from DNA.
Culture, continuity and regionally variable replacement GAC continuity and geographically variable Beaker ancestry show that similar material culture can spread without equivalent population movement, while other regions experienced major replacement. Tassi et al. (2017); Olalde et al. (2018); Papac et al. (2021) The strongest Beaker replacement evidence is later than the core Yamnaya interval and should not be projected backward. Strong case studies in northwestern Poland, central Europe and Britain; not a universal Eurasian pattern. Pre-target continuity plus late-target and post-target consequences Tassi explicitly evaluates language-dispersal models; Olalde and Papac contribute indirect demographic constraints. More matched archaeological–genetic regional transects are needed to separate cultural transmission, elite dominance and population replacement. RETAIN — recurring 3 Migration qualifier Tests whether material-culture change corresponds to demographic movement; culture is included only as a migration qualifier.
Caucasus and northern Eurasian context Long-term Steppe–Caucasus and Siberia–northeastern Europe gene flow complicates a single-origin, single-wave narrative and supplies context for Yamnaya ancestry formation. Dzhaubermezov et al. (2026); Wong et al. (2017); Lazaridis et al. (2025); Nikitin et al. (2025) Two contributions are secondary or modern-population-led, and their broad chronology does not isolate direct Yamnaya movement. Uneven: growing Steppe data, sparse early North Caucasus/lower-Volga coverage and indirect northern Eurasian sampling. Mainly earlier ancestry context, with Bronze Age corridor evidence Mostly background; explicit language reconstruction comes primarily from Lazaridis rather than the contextual studies. Independent ancient samples from the North Caucasus, lower Volga and northern Eurasian corridors remain limited. RETAIN — recurring 4 Migration context Retains earlier and broader gene flow only as context needed to interpret target-period Yamnaya movement across Eurasia.
Migration and Indo-European language dispersal The corpus repeatedly treats rapid Steppe-related population spread as a plausible demographic mechanism compatible with Indo-European dispersal. Lazaridis et al. (2025); Haak et al. (2015); Allentoft et al. (2015); Wang et al. (2021); Chintalapati et al. (2022); Tassi et al. (2017) Ancient DNA cannot reveal speech; migration need not cause complete language replacement, and language can spread without equivalent genetic turnover. Demographic evidence is strongest in the Steppe and central Europe; linguistic linkage is weakest where independent linguistic evidence is absent. Mixed: earlier language-root hypotheses, target-period dispersal and later branch consequences EXPLICIT: 6; INDIRECT: 7; BACKGROUND: 2. All are interpretations, not language observed in DNA. The collection contains no direct linguistic dataset; branching, borrowing, multilingualism and transmission mechanisms require independent linguistic evidence. RETAIN — recurring 6 Language synthesis Separates direct genetic/demographic findings from authorial or interdisciplinary linguistic interpretation in every claim.
